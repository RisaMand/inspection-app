# The Project Log

*Everything that actually happened, in order, told straight.*

This isn't a changelog in the boring sense — dates and diffs nobody reads. It's the record of every real decision this project made, every time it caught itself doing something dumb and fixed it, and every fork in the road where "the easy thing" and "the right thing" were two different options. Commit hashes are included wherever they exist, so nothing here is just a story — you can go check.

For a long stretch, this log only had the frontend, the PWA shell, and document generation to talk about, because that was genuinely the whole story at the time — the two hardest pieces of this system, computer vision and the legal rule-engine, belonged to two teammates who had gone quiet, off building in their own folders, tested against nothing but their own mock data, never once introduced to the actual app. A fourth piece — a real backend — was also being built during that same stretch, by a fourth person, but deliberately kept out of sight on purpose: rather than chase three moving, unfinished pieces at once, the team held the backend back until the first two had actually been made to work together. Everything from here on is the record of those pieces arriving, one at a time, in the order they actually did — not patched in after the fact, but told exactly where they land.

---

## Part 0 — The decisions made before a single line of code existed

Every project has a shape before it has a body. Here's the shape this one was given, and why.

**It's a PWA, not an app-store app, not a website.** That sounds like a small choice. It isn't. A field inspector needs something that lives on their phone's home screen, opens the camera without ceremony, and keeps working when the signal doesn't — without anyone having to survive an app-store review process first. A Progressive Web App gets you all of that for the cost of a website. The alternative — a native Android build — was seriously on the table and deliberately set aside.

**Everything runs on the phone. Nothing waits on a server.** An earlier draft of this project's architecture leaned on a heavier setup — a real backend, jobs queued up, results polled for. That got thrown out. If an inspector is standing in a shop with one bar of signal, "please wait while we process this" is not an acceptable sentence. So the camera, the quality checks, and eventually the OCR and rule-checking are all meant to happen right there, in the browser, fast enough that nothing ever needs a spinner that says "processing."

**Offline is not a feature bolted on later — it's the foundation.** Everything that persists lives in IndexedDB, the browser's real local database, not the flimsier `localStorage` that can't reliably hold photos and forgets things it shouldn't.

**The law lives in data, not in code.** Whatever eventually checks a label against the actual Legal Metrology rules will read those rules from a versioned config file, never hardcode them into application logic. That's someone else's build, still to come — but it shaped how every screen here was written to accept *whatever* verdict comes back, generically, instead of being wired to expect one specific shape.

**Four things make this different, and they were earned, not assumed.** Before any of this got built, real competitive research went into what already exists — a prior national-competition winner's hardware-and-dashboard approach, a consumer-complaint app, adjacent open-source work. Out of that came four deliberate bets on where this project is actually different: it reads labels that mix English, Hindi, and regional scripts on the same package (most tools choke on this); it's built for an *inspector's* workflow, not a shopper's; it specifically hunts for a real, legally-defined fraud — a sticker pasted over the true price; and it tells the difference between a font-size slip-up and a genuinely missing MRP, instead of a flat pass/fail. These four ideas show up again and again in how screens were designed, because they weren't slogans — they were commitments.

**The team, honestly.** Six people are attached to this project on paper. As of the earliest working sessions, one of them had produced working code. A second was quietly building the backend in parallel — real work, happening the whole time — but deliberately asked to hold it back and stay out of the way rather than hand it over immediately. The reasoning was simple: the two hardest, most independent pieces of the whole system — computer vision and the rule-engine — hadn't even met this app yet, let alone each other, and introducing a third unfinished dependency into that mess would have meant nobody could tell which of three things was actually broken when something failed. Better to get two islands talking first, cleanly, and only then bring in the third.

**Two different kinds of "how bad is this violation" got kept separate, on purpose.** Whether a label breaks the law is a legal question. How urgently an inspector with forty products and one hour should care about it is a completely different, operational question. Rather than force those into one flattened field, the project kept them as two — a legal severity, and an inspection priority — because collapsing them would have quietly smuggled a legal opinion into a UI decision.

**Uncertain evidence gets to say "I don't know."** Long before any of the visual fraud-detection logic existed, the interface for it was scoped to four honest states, not two: a confirmed violation, a likely one, a perfectly legal pattern that must never *look* suspicious, and — critically — a state for when a sticker fully hides the original price and the system genuinely cannot tell what's underneath. That fourth state matters more than it sounds like it should. A tool that quietly defaults uncertainty into "pass" is a tool that lies by omission.

**No raw machine numbers in front of an inspector.** A confidence score of `0.87` means nothing to someone standing in a shop. Anything like that gets translated into plain language — "review this photo" instead of a percentage — before it's allowed anywhere near the primary screen. The number can live in a details panel for anyone who wants it. It never gets to be the headline.

## Part 1 — Laying the floor

The very first version of this app was, deliberately, almost embarrassingly simple: a login screen where any password works, a form to start a visit, and a camera that takes a photo. That simplicity was the point. Before anything clever could be built, something *honest and swappable* had to exist first.

The fake login wasn't laziness — it was a seam, built on purpose. One small function pretends to check a password today. Replacing it later with a real check means touching that one function, not rewiring the whole app around it. That pattern — mock something small and contained now, leave a clean seam for the real thing later — became the project's signature move, used again and again for every piece that wasn't ready yet.

From there, the actual inspector's journey got built piece by piece: a real login form with role-aware routing, a real "start a visit" screen with GPS capture and a manual fallback for when permission gets denied, and — the meatiest piece — a real capture screen. Live camera. Multiple photos per item, with thumbnails you could remove. A fake quality-check standing in for the computer vision that didn't exist yet, flagged in plain comments as something that *must* be swapped before this ever meets a real judge. A file-upload fallback for when the camera isn't the right tool. A barcode-scan button that, refreshingly, just told the truth: *not built yet*, instead of pretending to work.

By the end of this stretch, photographs an inspector took were actually landing in a real session record, and the whole thing was safely pushed to a personal GitHub repository — a small, unglamorous, genuinely important decision, made explicitly so that nine commits of real, working code weren't one bad afternoon away from vanishing.

## Part 2 — Making it feel like a real tool

With the skeleton standing, the next stretch was about turning "a few working screens" into something that could survive being handed to a stranger.

Login and session state got moved out of fragile browser memory and into IndexedDB, so a refresh — or a dropped connection, or a phone that decided to nap — stopped meaning "start over." A sync-status indicator was added, honestly labeled as counting *locally saved* items rather than confirmed server syncs, because there was no server yet to sync to — a small piece of honesty that got explicitly debated and deliberately kept, on the reasoning that once a real backend exists, the same label becomes true without a single word needing to change.

The Item Report and Consolidated Visit Report screens came together next, and they were built to render *only real data* — actual photos, actual visit and shop details — with an unapologetic "compliance check pending" message everywhere a verdict would eventually go. No invented pass/fail. No pretend confidence. Both report types could be exported as real PDFs with embedded evidence photos, and shortly after, as editable Word documents too — closing a promise that had been sitting unfulfilled since the very beginning.

The seizure memo — the single highest-stakes document this system can produce — got its skeleton here too: a genuine draft-versus-confirmed toggle, because a legal notice that looks final before a human has actually signed off on it is a liability, not a feature. Every field inside it was marked plainly as waiting on the rule-engine, rather than filled with anything invented.

Somewhere in here, the app also stopped being a collection of screens you had to reach by typing URLs by hand, and became something you could actually click through start to finish: capture an item, see its report, scan the next one, close out the visit. It sounds small. It's the difference between a tech demo and a tool.

## Part 3 — The reckoning

This is the long stretch, and it deserves to be told honestly, because it's where this project stopped being merely functional and started being *trustworthy*.

A hard rule got set at the start of it: no more building forward, no talking about the pieces still waiting on other people, until this thing had been tested by fire — repeatedly, deliberately, looking for every gap, not just the obvious ones — with nothing left unexamined.

**The first thing the fire found was embarrassing.** Role-based access had been wired up, screens gated, everything looking correct — except the one piece of data that made the gate actually work had never been passed through. Every single login, regardless of role, was quietly landing on the same screen. A one-line fix. A real lesson about the gap between "looks right" and "is right."

**Then it found something worse than a bug — an absence.** There was no way to log out. None. And worse: the login screen had no idea whether someone was already logged in, meaning a second person could type one URL and silently hijack an active session mid-work, no warning, no trace. Both got fixed together — a real Log Out button, and a login screen that now recognizes when it's being visited by someone who's already signed in.

**Then it found something that would have been genuinely dangerous.** Deny the camera permission — deliberately, as a test — and the capture button kept working anyway, quietly producing a *black, empty photograph* and letting it flow into the evidence trail like it was real. For a tool whose entire purpose is producing legally defensible evidence, a fake photo masquerading as a real one is about as bad as bugs get. It's fixed now — no stream, no capture, full stop, with a clear message instead of a silent lie.

**Manual GPS entry accepted nonsense.** Letters where numbers should be. No range checking. A field meant to record a real location happily swallowed garbage and would have written it straight into a legal record. Fixed with real number constraints and honest range validation — while still letting a genuinely empty field mean exactly what it should: *not captured*, not *invented*.

**Nothing stopped an item from collecting fifty photographs, or a visit from swallowing hundreds of them.** Not a theoretical risk — every photo is a real, full-sized image, held in memory, written to local storage, and eventually baked into a PDF. A two-tier answer was chosen deliberately: gently warn an inspector piling up photos on one item, because there's rarely a real reason to need more than a handful of angles on one product — but only *nudge*, never block, on the number of items in a single visit, because a real inspection could legitimately need forty.

**Then the search got more serious, and it found four real, separate things in one pass.** The camera was quietly staying on after leaving the capture screen — genuinely, physically, the little indicator light stayed lit — because of a subtle timing bug in how the camera stream was being tracked and released, made worse by a development quirk that briefly opens *two* camera streams instead of one. It took a few tries to actually pin down, and it wasn't trusted as fixed until the physical light on the laptop was watched turning off in real time — because a log claiming success and a camera that's still visibly on are not the same thing, and only one of them can be believed.

In the same pass: photographs captured but never finished as an item were being saved under one shared, unscoped slot — meaning an abandoned draft from one visit could silently reappear, misattributed, inside an entirely different one later. And every uploaded photo that wasn't already a plain JPEG — anything from an iPhone, anything saved as a PNG or WEBP — was quietly being mislabeled in the exported documents, a real risk of corrupted or broken evidence images in the one place they absolutely cannot fail: the final report. Both were fixed at the root, not patched around.

**And then the fourth thing in that pass turned out to be the whole rest of the story.**

## Part 4 — The session that ate the day

What started as "make sure two people sharing a device can't step on each other's work" turned into the most significant architectural rebuild in this project's history, and it's worth walking through slowly, because the reasoning matters more than the fix.

The first instinct was small: tag each visit with whoever started it, and warn if someone else's login stumbled into it. That got tested against reality and found wanting almost immediately — a warning that only fires on one specific button doesn't help if there are five other doors into the same room. So the next instinct was to guard *every* door — every screen, every route. But that just moved the real problem one step over: the moment a second person is blocked from someone else's unfinished visit, *something* still has to happen to that visit. Force them to log out and lose their own moment of momentum? Or let them past — but at the cost of quietly destroying the first person's evidence?

Both of those were the same bad trade wearing different clothes. And the reason kept being the same bad trade, no matter how the warning dialog was reworded, was the actual root of it: there was only ever **one slot** for a visit to live in. With one slot, somebody's data was always going to lose.

So the fix wasn't a smarter dialog box. It was getting rid of the scarcity entirely. Visits stopped being one overwritable record and became a real, permanent, tagged list — every visit remembers who made it, and ending a visit now means *archiving* it, not deleting it. Two different logins on the same device now simply don't collide, because there's finally room for both of them to exist.

That meant solving a smaller, sharper question first: who actually *is* "a person" in a system where the login is fake and any password works? The tempting shortcut — tag visits by username and password together — got tossed out almost as soon as it was proposed, and for a reason worth keeping: the password isn't checked against anything real, so it adds no genuine uniqueness, and there's no good reason to ever store a password anywhere, even a fake one nobody's verifying. What actually solved it: the first time any username shows up, it's given a real, permanent, randomly generated identity behind the scenes — and every visit is tagged with *that*, not with anything typed into a form. No password touches the database. Not once.

One more real fork came up along the way: should a person be able to have several visits open at once, switching between them freely? It's tempting, and it maps to how a real inspector's day might actually feel. But it was set aside deliberately, in favor of something narrower and more honest: one open visit at a time, with a permanent, retrievable history of everything that came before. That choice traded a flashier feature for something more important — it closes off an entire category of "which visit was I even looking at" mistakes before they can happen, and it satisfies, almost as a side effect, a real requirement this system was always going to need anyway: a genuine, searchable record of past inspections.

The rebuild itself happened in five careful, individually verified stages — the new storage shape first, then real identity, then the actual redesign of how a visit gets found and updated, then a small but important correction to a warning message that had quietly become a lie the moment archiving replaced deleting, and finally wiring the real identity through to make all of it live.

And even after all five stages landed, testing it end-to-end — really testing it, not just glancing at it — turned up two more real problems hiding in the wiring. A couple of functions had quietly become asynchronous partway through the rebuild, and a couple of call sites hadn't caught up, which meant a freshly finished item briefly tried to navigate to a web address that was, quite literally, the word "Promise" instead of a real ID. And choosing to deliberately override an unfinished visit — the "yes, start fresh anyway" path — was creating a second, silently simultaneous "active" visit instead of properly closing out the first, which would have reopened a smaller version of the exact ambiguity this whole rebuild existed to kill. Both were caught by refusing to accept "it looks like it worked" as good enough, and both are fixed and proven now — not with a glance, but with two different logins, run through a full visit each, on a clean and deliberately reset database, checked directly against what was actually sitting in storage afterward.

That's the difference between a bug getting patched and a system actually earning trust.

## Part 5 — Meeting the real device

Every single test up to this point had happened on a laptop. That's a comfortable place to build, and a genuinely misleading one — because the actual person this app is for will never once open it on a laptop.

Testing on a real phone turned out to be its own small adventure. The obvious first attempt — same Wi-Fi network, laptop and phone talking directly — ran straight into a wall that had nothing to do with this app's code: browsers simply refuse to hand over a camera or a location fix to anything that isn't served securely, and a plain local network address doesn't count as secure, no matter how private it feels. No error, no permission prompt, no explanation — just silence, exactly where a working feature should have been.

The fix was to get this thing a real, secure address on the actual internet — a small, unglamorous deployment step that turned out to be the only way to genuinely test a camera on a phone at all. And the very first real result was exactly what had been quietly predicted beforehand: the camera opened facing the wrong way. A selfie camera, on a tool built for photographing products, would have been a visibly bad first impression in front of anyone judging this live. It's fixed now — the rear camera is requested explicitly, with a safe fallback for laptops and any device that only has the one — and it wasn't trusted as fixed on faith. It was confirmed live, on an actual Android phone, camera pointed the right way, before it was allowed to be called done.

As a genuinely nice surprise along the way: the app installed cleanly to a real phone's home screen as its own icon, proof that the Progressive Web App groundwork laid at the very start of this project actually works, not just in theory.

## Part 6 — Two islands, one system

For five parts, this log has been honest about a hole in the middle of the project: it could photograph a label beautifully and had absolutely no idea what the label said or whether it broke the law. That hole wasn't neglect — it was waiting on two other people's work. Person 3 had a real computer-vision and OCR pipeline. Person 4 had a real rule-engine, config-driven, legally reasoned. Both existed. Neither had ever been introduced to the app, or to each other. This session was the introduction, and it went about as well as a first meeting between two strangers who'd each been assured the other one was "basically compatible" usually goes — which is to say: mostly fine, with a few genuinely alarming moments in the middle.

**R1 and R2 — moving two strangers into the same house.** The OCR module went in first: `ocr1.0` and `ocr2.0` got merged into a single best-of-both `src/lib/cv/index.js`, with the older `blurCheck.js` retired to an `_archive/` folder rather than deleted outright — a small act of institutional memory, in case anything downstream still quietly expected it to exist. The rule-engine module came in right behind it, and immediately tripped over the kind of landmine that only exists because two people worked on two different operating systems: Person 4 had named the config file `Ruleconfig.json` — capital R, lowercase c — while every file that imported it asked for `ruleConfig.json`, lowercase r, capital C. Windows doesn't care about that distinction. Linux, and every CI runner and deploy target this project will ever touch, cares enormously. That one got caught here, on a laptop, before it ever had the chance to fail silently in front of a judge — which is exactly the point of catching it here.

The bigger surprise was underneath: Person 4's entire rule-engine subtree had been written in CommonJS (`module.exports`), while this app runs as native ES Modules end to end. Not a typo — an entirely different module system, applied consistently, by someone who'd never needed to think about how their code would eventually be `import`ed into a Vite app rather than `require`d into a Node script. Converting it wasn't optional and wasn't cosmetic: `ruleInterpreter.js` and `verdictEvaluator.js` both got their exports rewritten to real ESM, and Person 4's own `test.js` — a genuinely well-built four-scenario test suite (fully compliant, a cosmetic font-size slip, a substantive missing-MRP failure, and a conditional country-of-origin rule that correctly skips itself when a product isn't imported) — got rewritten around it, loading its JSON config through `fs.readFileSync` and `fileURLToPath` rather than a bare import, because Node still won't let ESM import JSON directly without extra ceremony. Once that was done, all four scenarios ran clean and matched their expected verdicts exactly. That test suite earned its keep here — it's the reason this rewrite could be trusted the moment it stopped throwing, instead of needing a second, slower round of manual poking.

**R3 — building the bridge, and finding the first real disagreement.** OCR output and rule-engine input don't speak the same language by default: one hands back a blob of raw multi-line text and a single whole-image confidence number, the other wants a dictionary keyed by exact field names (`MRP`, `NET_QUANTITY`, `MANUFACTURER_ADDRESS`, and so on), each with its own confidence score. `src/lib/mapFieldsToRules.js` is the translator — a synonym table (`net qty` / `net wt` / `net weight` all mean the same field; `mfg` / `mfd` / `packed on` all mean another) that scans the OCR text line by line and slots whatever it finds into the shape the rule engine expects. It's explicitly, honestly scoped as an R1 best-effort: one confidence number standing in for what should eventually be per-field confidence, because there's no bounding-box extraction yet to produce the real thing.

Testing the bridge against a real Indian-packaging-style string surfaced the first genuine architectural disagreement between the two modules: Person 4's `MRP_FORMAT` regex had been calibrated against exactly one string — Person 4's own synthetic test data, `MRP ₹120` — and broke the instant it met anything a real label would actually say, like `MRP Rs 120 incl. of all taxes`. That's not a bug in the wiring; the pipeline ran clean, start to finish, without throwing. It's a brittleness in someone else's business logic, and it got named as exactly that rather than quietly patched around — because the job in this session was integration, not rewriting Person 4's regex on their behalf. It was committed as-is, flagged plainly, left for Person 4 to fix on their own module.

**R4 — where OpenCV.js picked a fight with the entire architecture.** `cv.imread()`, it turns out, is opinionated in a very specific and very unhelpful way: in the browser it will only accept an element that's actually mounted in the DOM — a real `<canvas>`, a real `<img>`, or a string naming one by ID. It flatly refuses a base64 data URL, and — this took three separate attempts to fully pin down — it also refuses a `<canvas>` created with `document.createElement('canvas')` that was never attached to the page, even though that looks, to a human, exactly like the kind of canvas it's supposed to accept. Each attempted fix solved the symptom in front of it and revealed the next one: pass the live camera's canvas ref → still fails on uploads → wrap uploads through an `Image` element drawn onto a temporary canvas → still fails, because "temporary" meant "detached." The fix that actually held was to stop asking `cv.imread()` to accept anything at all: pull the raw pixel buffer out with `getImageData()` and hand it to `cv.matFromImageData()` instead, which never touches the DOM and therefore has no DOM requirement to violate.

That fix went into `qualityCheck.js` — and then the identical error came back five minutes later, from a completely different file. `preprocess.js`, written independently by Person 3 for the OCR preparation step, had its own unpatched `cv.imread(imageElementOrCanvas)` call, one function further down the same pipeline. Same bug, same root cause, different address. The same DOM-avoidance helper had to be built a second time, inside `preprocess.js`, before a photo could survive the trip from capture to a clean OCR read without crashing partway through. It's a small, sharp lesson in why "fix it in one place" isn't the same as "fix it" when two people wrote two files that happen to call the same finicky library function.

**R5 — the pipeline goes live, and immediately looks like it didn't.** `useSession.js`'s `addItem()` had been hardcoding `checkResult: null` since the very beginning, as an honest placeholder. That line was replaced with the real three-step pipeline — `mapFieldsToRules` → `checkCompliance` → `evaluateVerdict` — wrapped in a `try/catch` so that a pipeline failure degrades to a visible `ERROR` verdict on screen rather than vanishing silently. It worked, on the first real test, and looked exactly like it hadn't: `ItemResult.jsx` kept showing "Compliance check pending" anyway. The cause was almost funny in hindsight — the screen's verdict-to-color map still only recognized the *old* placeholder vocabulary (`PASS`, `REVIEW`), while the real rule engine speaks a completely different one (`COMPLIANT`, `COMPLIANT_WITH_WARNINGS`, `NON_COMPLIANT`). A working pipeline was quietly falling through to the "unrecognized verdict" default every single time. Fixed by teaching the screen the engine's actual vocabulary, and — just as importantly — deleting the leftover hardcoded "Rule Engine not yet integrated" sentence sitting right next to it, which by that point had quietly become a lie every time it rendered.

`ConsolidatedReport.jsx` and `SeizureMemoReview.jsx` got the same treatment in the same pass — both had been carrying their own "[Pending — integration not yet done]" placeholders since Part 2 of this very log, and both now render real verdict badges, real clause citations, and real extracted fields, on-screen and inside the PDF and Word exports alike. The seizure memo in particular had a second problem hiding underneath the first: it wasn't reachable from anywhere in the actual app — no button pointed at it, only a manually-typed `/seizure-memo` in the address bar — and when the route was actually inspected, `App.jsx` wasn't even passing it the `session` prop it needed to read GPS, visit number, or which items had failed. Both are fixed now: the prop is wired through, and a real "Review Seizure Memo" button lives on the consolidated report, alongside a conditional "Draft Seizure Memo" shortcut that only appears on `ItemResult.jsx` when that specific item comes back `NON_COMPLIANT`.

**R6 — the build fights back, one more time.** Bundling OpenCV.js's WebAssembly runtime directly into the app pushed the main JS chunk to 16.6 MB, and Workbox — the service-worker tool responsible for offline caching — has a default 2 MB precache ceiling that treats anything larger as a hard build failure, not a warning. `npm run build` died on it outright. The fix was a single explicit line in `vite.config.js`, raising `workbox.maximumFileSizeToCacheInBytes` to 25 MB inside the existing PWA config — and once that was in, the build went clean, and `npm run preview` confirmed the production bundle actually serves.

What R6 did *not* get to, by deliberate choice rather than running out of steam: the two structured real-product test cases this step was named for — one deliberately clean label confirmed as `COMPLIANT`, one deliberately incomplete label confirmed as `NON_COMPLIANT` with the correct clause citation, plus an offline-mode check with the service worker toggled on and the network switched off. All three were named, scoped, and explicitly parked for a later session, alongside a file-cleanup pass — a pair of scratch integration files this step had leaned on, sitting unused since — that likewise got parked rather than handled on the spot.

And somewhere in the middle of all of it, a small, real act of team maintenance: once the pipeline was live, two short notes went out — one to Person 3, one to Person 4 — naming the exact technical decisions in their modules that made this integration go as smoothly as it did: Person 3's choice to pin Tesseract's page-segmentation mode via `worker.setParameters()` inside worker initialization rather than as a loose per-call option (which quietly gets dropped on handheld shots if you don't), and the adaptive-threshold preprocessing chain that feeds it cleaner input; Person 4's choice to keep `ruleInterpreter.js` dispatching purely on `check_type` instead of hardcoding individual rule IDs, and the cosmetic-versus-substantive verdict tiering that maps directly onto how a real seizure memo needs to read. Specific, short, and true — the kind of appreciation that's actually useful to the person receiving it, because it tells them exactly which of their instincts to keep trusting.

The core loop — log in, start a visit, photograph products, close it out, get a real report — worked now, was safe on a shared device, survived a dropped connection, and had been tested against its own worst-case scenarios more than once. A label captured in `Capture.jsx` genuinely got read, by a real Tesseract OCR pipeline, and genuinely got judged, by a real config-driven rule engine, live, in the browser, flowing straight through into the consolidated report and the seizure memo. What was still honestly missing, and still said so: font-size and on-panel placement checks, filtered out of every evaluation because they needed bounding-box data this OCR pass didn't produce yet; multilingual OCR beyond a single language; sticker/price-tamper detection; the two structured verification test cases still parked; and the fourth piece, the backend, still sitting exactly where it had been asked to sit — out of the way, quietly being built, waiting for its turn.

## Part 7 — The fourth piece comes out of hiding

With the CV pipeline and the rule engine finally talking to each other, the reason for keeping the backend on the sidelines no longer applied, and it stopped being held back. A full audit was run first, of the entire real state of the project — not any one person's folder, all of them — against every governing document the team had written for itself: the Team Build Bible, the atomic mapping, the pipeline description, the official problem-statement text. It found the frontend's own claims about itself checked out exactly, line for line, against the real committed code. It also found one live, still-unfixed bug flagged as the single highest-probability point of failure in front of a judge — the same brittle `MRP_FORMAT` regex Part 6 had already named and left for Person 4 — and one real structural tension nobody had reconciled on paper: the team's own planning timeline had been written assuming a project of one, and it was now, again, a project of four active people, with nobody having gone back to update the plan to say so.

Four new documents came out of that audit, one per active person, each scoped to override the team's earlier, more conservative planning documents rather than sit alongside them — a frozen, explicit description of the exact data shapes crossing each pair of role boundaries, so nobody built the next piece against a guess; an honest read of what actually existed in that person's own domain on the real, current branch; a concrete, ordered list of what to build next; and a section spelling out precisely what each person needed from the others, and what the others needed back. Three things were flagged as load-bearing across all four documents at once: the MRP-format fix, sitting above everything else in urgency; the backend being the most consequential of the four, since the dashboard, the rule-config versioning, and the official problem statement's explicit requirement for "a repository of scanned products and compliance history" were all structurally blocked on it existing at all; and one precise, one-line handoff between the CV side and the rule-engine side — the exact moment a real field-detection pass started reliably reporting font size and on-page position for each extracted field, two permanently-disabled legal checks would go live with the deletion of exactly one filter line. The same failure this project had already once lived through — two modules built in complete isolation, their mismatch discovered only when they finally met — was named explicitly as the thing to avoid a second time.

Person 2's backend, once it surfaced, turned out to be a real, standalone repository — not a branch of the shared project, a separate one entirely, under his own account. Every file in it got read, not skimmed: every route, every controller, every validator, the actual database migration, the actual test suite. What held up without qualification, on a first honest look: real idempotency-key handling with payload-hash comparison and optimistic locking on a `server_version` column, exactly the kind of conflict-safe offline sync this project's founding offline-first principle demanded; bcrypt hashing, real JWT issuance, parameterized SQL with no injection surface anywhere, per-request schema validation, and genuine ownership isolation, confirmed by actually trying to read another inspector's record and being correctly refused; a real database constraint enforcing exactly one active rule-config version at a time, the specific integrity guarantee this project's own Build Bible calls non-negotiable, built as an actual constraint rather than a promise in application code; and, worth naming on its own, two of the backend's biggest open architectural questions disclosed in its own documentation, in writing, rather than guessed at silently and shipped.

What didn't hold up was serious, and one gap sat above the rest. The Build Bible's own schema calls for a `Sessions` table — a visit, holding a visit number, a shop number, GPS coordinates, and containing multiple items within it. The real, shipped schema had no such table anywhere — a single flat `inspections` row per scanned product, with no concept of a visit grouping anything together at all. That meant the project's own, already-working Consolidated Visit Report had nothing on the backend side to eventually sync against; there was no visit for it to belong to. Sitting beside it: no `Products` table and no barcode or violation-history endpoint at all, against the official problem statement's own explicit requirement to maintain a repository of scanned products and their compliance history; no dashboard search endpoint at all, against that same document's equally explicit requirement for a search and retrieval facility; a rule engine modeled as if it were a separate, external service — an evaluation-status column cycling through pending and evaluated states, a separate endpoint gated to an administrator role to receive a result later — when the real, already-shipped rule engine runs entirely on-device, synchronously, the instant a photo is captured, with no separate service anywhere for that architecture to describe. Two smaller mismatches sat alongside these: the endpoint meant to receive a compliance result expected a shape with field names that didn't exist anywhere in the real, shipped verdict object; and the schema stored the declared MRP as a parsed number, discarding the exact raw text the real MRP-format rule actually needs to check for a currency symbol and "inclusive of all taxes" phrasing.

An atomic fix list went back — genuine appreciation, named specifically rather than generically, for what had actually been built well, followed by every gap, each with the exact file and the exact missing piece, split cleanly into what was safe to build immediately without further discussion and what needed the whole team to actually agree on something first, not one person guessing on everyone's behalf. Two decisions were held back for that reason specifically: how the rule engine's already-computed result should actually reach the sync payload, given there was no separate service for it to arrive from the way the schema assumed; and where evidence photos were supposed to live at all, since the backend's documented contract expected an already-uploaded image reference and nothing anywhere in the whole system, frontend or backend, had ever built an actual upload step. Photos existed, and still exist, as data inside a browser's IndexedDB, nowhere else.

## Part 8 — The backend, reviewed live, twice

Person 2 came back with a real pull request — not to his own standalone repository this time, but a proper branch merging into the shared project, fifty files. It was reviewed the same way as the first pass: cloned fresh, a real PostgreSQL instance stood up from nothing, every migration run against it in order, seed data loaded, and every previously broken call hit directly, live, with real requests rather than trusted from whatever the pull request's own description claimed.

That distinction mattered immediately. The existing test suite reported every one of its assertions passing — and reading the actual bodies of those assertions showed the overwhelming majority were nothing more than a literal `true` checked against itself, a suite that had never once exercised the code it claimed to cover. Running the real thing instead of trusting that green output surfaced the single most severe backend bug this project has found: the request-validation middleware unconditionally overwrote a request's route parameters and query string with whatever a validation schema happened to parse, and any schema that declared only a request body — which several of the newest, most important ones did — implicitly wiped those parameters down to nothing in the process. Three genuinely central endpoints — updating an inspection, submitting one for review, and attaching a compliance result to one — crashed outright on every single call, confirmed live, one after another, against a real database. It sat above every other item on the list, because nothing else on the list mattered if these three didn't work at all.

That exact bug was genuinely fixed in this same pull request, and it was verified the same way it had been found: real calls, a real 200 response, a real version-conflict counter incrementing correctly, a real status transition through the correct sequence of states. Alongside it, real progress landed on several other fronts — a genuine `sessions` table and a genuine `products` table now existed at all, where before there had been none; dashboard search returned real, correct matches against real seeded data; the dashboard's own compliance-count summary stopped being a hardcoded zero and began aggregating something real; and the test suite itself was rebuilt from a dozen empty assertions into fifteen genuine ones, run against an in-memory Postgres, directly exercising the exact flow that had been crashing before.

Not everything closed cleanly, and each remaining gap got the same live-verification treatment rather than being taken on the pull request's own word. The new `sessions` table, despite finally existing, still had no visit number, no shop number, and no GPS columns at all — the exact data this project's own frontend had been capturing from the very beginning had nowhere on the backend to actually go. A constraint requiring every product to have a scanned barcode meant that, with barcode scanning still an unbuilt feature anywhere in the system, almost no real capture could ever be linked into the new products table at all. The sync endpoint's own documented guarantee — that one malformed record shouldn't sink an entire batch of synced work — turned out to hold only for a narrow set of expected failures, because the whole batch still ran inside a single database transaction that rolled back completely on anything genuinely unexpected. The compliance-result endpoint's schema had been made more permissive rather than actually corrected, letting the real payload through by accident of loosened validation rather than by declaring the real shape on purpose. And the rule-config workflow — create a new version, then activate it — turned out, live, to be a genuine dead end: the endpoint that creates a new configuration never returns its own database identifier at all, and the endpoint that activates one requires exactly that identifier, with a comment left directly in the shipped code by its own author, admitting uncertainty about which kind of identifier to use, and guessing.

The recommendation, and the decision, was to merge anyway. It was purely additive, the one true blocking defect was genuinely and verifiably fixed, and nothing left open put any existing frontend feature at risk by staying open a while longer. The merge itself was made to leave its own permanent, readable trail directly on the project rather than living only in a conversation: a detailed comment posted on the pull request itself, naming everything confirmed fixed and everything still open with the evidence behind each claim; the merge; and a tracked issue on the repository holding the full remaining list, so the next round of work had a real, permanent home rather than a document nobody would think to reopen. A short addendum went out separately not long after, once real dashboard work actually began against this backend's endpoints and surfaced two more gaps the original review hadn't reached: an endpoint for listing sessions that was correctly scoped by role in principle, but gated in a way that made it entirely unreachable to the one role — the official's — that actually needed to read it; and an officer-activity endpoint that returned nothing but a bare list of inspector accounts, with no aggregation of visits, violations, or severity breakdown behind any of it at all.

## Part 9 — A dashboard, built against a promise instead of a database

The decision that followed was deliberate and stated plainly at the time: rather than wait for the backend's remaining gaps to close before building anything the official side of this app needed, the whole dashboard would be built now, real and complete as user-facing screens, against data that was entirely made up — but made up honestly, shaped exactly like the backend's real, corrected contracts, so that the eventual swap from invented data to a live network call would be a small, contained change to what lived *inside* a handful of functions, never a change to how any screen called them.

That mock dataset came first, on its own, checked before a single screen was built against it: eight visits, twelve scanned items, four inspectors, four shops, spread across roughly a month — every compliance result shaped exactly like the real, shipped verdict-evaluator output, every rule identifier and clause citation pulled from the real rule configuration rather than invented, and one real schema gap named rather than quietly worked around: the backend's `sessions` table, even in its corrected form, has no product-category column at all, so category was deliberately dropped as a filter dimension across the whole mock module, rather than left as a filter control with nothing real behind it. On top of that dataset, a set of query functions were designed against the pipeline's own real, ugly edge case, checked in advance rather than discovered by accident: the app's own error-handling path produces a genuine failed-evaluation result with none of the usual extracted fields at all when the on-device pipeline throws partway through, a real production path rather than a hypothetical one, and every function touching extracted data was built to degrade gracefully against exactly that shape.

Five real screens went up on top of that foundation, each verified against its own real numbers before being called finished — a discipline that caught two genuine problems mid-build rather than after. A summary screen shipped eight real cards, a violation-tier chart, and a trend chart with a selectable time window, checked not just by loading it once but by actually switching that window and confirming the chart visibly redrew with a different point count, since loading a page once proves nothing about whether an interaction actually works. A filter-and-drilldown screen shipped real filtering by date range, inspector, shop, and severity tier, cross-checked by hand against known combinations — including confirming that one specific combination legitimately returning zero results was the correct, boring behavior, not a bug worth chasing. A search screen shipped real, unrestricted matching across visit number, shop, inspector name, and product name. A report viewer reused the exact same presentational component this project had already extracted once, back during the integration work in Part 6, for precisely this kind of reuse — real proof that the extraction had paid for itself the first time it was asked to. And an officer-activity screen shipped a stacked bar chart, substantive violations stacked against cosmetic ones per inspector, checked directly against the raw numbers sitting beneath it to confirm the bar heights genuinely matched the data rather than merely existing next to it.

The very last piece of this stretch — exporting a filtered result set to PDF, and separately the entire dataset unfiltered — is where a real bug got caught the same way this project has learned to catch things: not by reading the code and trusting it compiled, but by opening the actual downloaded file and looking at it. The first attempt produced a document with the right item count, the right visit and shop headers, and the right violation citations, but a header missing the inspector's name, the product name, and the date the on-screen table already showed, and every single embedded evidence photo simply absent, with no visible error anywhere to explain why. The cause, once traced, was specific: the mock dataset's placeholder photographs were SVG images, entirely legitimate for a browser to render on screen, and the PDF library's image-embedding function silently refused anything but real raster image bytes, failing inside a safety net that had originally been written to protect the export from one bad photo — and had, in the process, hidden the fact that every single photo was failing the exact same way. The fix reused a technique this project had already built once, in Part 3, for normalizing a real captured photo's file format before embedding it — extended here to also cover SVG, something a real camera would never produce but this synthetic dataset legitimately did — and the missing header fields were fixed by reusing the same row-construction logic the on-screen table already relied on, rather than deriving the same fields a second time and risking the two drifting apart later. The second export, covering the whole unfiltered dataset, was written with that fix already in place from the start, and needed no second round of debugging at all — the clearest evidence available that the first fix had actually been understood, not merely patched around.

## Part 10 — A visit gets a real shape, and a login gets a real seam

A third piece of backend work landed separately, on its own branch, and closed the single largest remaining gap from Part 8: the `sessions` table finally gained a real visit number, a real shop number, and real GPS coordinates — the exact fields this project's own frontend had been capturing since Part 1, with nowhere on a real backend to go until now. It was reviewed the same way as everything before it, merged cleanly, and confirmed live.

With that in place, the frontend's login stopped being safe to leave fake. A real, thin client got built for talking to the backend — a small wrapper that unwraps the server's own response shape and throws a real, informative error rather than swallowing a failure silently. The authentication hook that had stood in for a real login since Part 1 was rewritten around it: the old hardcoded, always-succeeding check disappeared entirely, replaced with a genuine call to the backend's login endpoint, a real token stored alongside the rest of the session state, and a login function that now hands back the account's real, server-decided role — a real bug was caught and fixed in the same pass, because an early draft of that function still returned a bare `true` or `false` rather than the actual role, which would have meant every single login routing to the same screen regardless of who had actually logged in, the exact bug this project had already once found and fixed the hard way, back in Part 3. Every one of these pieces was verified the way everything in this project has been verified since Part 3 — not by reading the code and trusting it, but by actually assembling the exact call chain in isolation and running it against a live backend, watching a real login, a real session creation, and a real session close all succeed in sequence with nothing mocked anywhere along the way. Running in parallel with all of the backend and dashboard work, a genuinely separate effort consumed its own share of attention: the official Idea-round presentation, held to the same standard as the code itself — nothing claimed that couldn't be backed up by something real, no invented number, no dressed-up guess.

## Part 12 — The night everything arrived at the same time

The last stretch before the pitch is the one where every open thread this log has been tracking separately converged into a single night, and it's worth telling in the order it actually happened, because the order is most of the point.

Two pull requests had been sitting unmerged for a while by this point: a large one from the CV/OCR side, nineteen commits deep, covering a genuine expansion of field-extraction accuracy and Hindi-language handling, and a smaller one from the backend side, fixing the sync validator's handling of newly created records and finally completing the report-data endpoint's field list. Both got reviewed live before either was approved. The backend one held up cleanly on every claim — a real test suite passing against a real database, and independent, hand-run confirmation on top of it that a session could genuinely be created without the version field the validator had wrongly required, that an update genuinely still required it, and that the report-data endpoint now genuinely returned every field it had been missing since Part 7. The larger one held up on almost everything — its own rule-engine tests passing, its lint clean, its production build succeeding — except for one real, concrete defect: a test file inside it imported a dependency through a hardcoded path to a specific folder on its own author's personal Windows machine, a path that could not possibly exist on any other computer, meaning its claimed full-green test result was true for exactly one machine on the planet. When the author's own response to that finding was that it was "just a test file" and therefore not a real issue, that reasoning was rejected directly and by name: a test file is exactly the kind of thing that gets pulled onto everyone else's machine the moment anyone merges the branch it lives on, and a suite that only proves itself on the one computer that wrote it isn't proving anything to anyone else — which is the entire reason a test suite exists in the first place. The branch was merged anyway, on the understanding that the one-line fix would be made and pushed back to the shared branch immediately, in the same sitting, rather than left as a private local patch that quietly diverged from what everyone else had just pulled.

Merging both branches meant pulling them down locally, and that pull ran straight into a real, if fully survivable, git problem: substantial local, uncommitted work — the login-and-session wiring from Part 10, still sitting unpushed on purpose — collided directly with incoming changes to some of the same files. The actual sequence that resolved it was stash the local work aside, pull the incoming changes cleanly, then bring the local work back and let a real three-way merge decide whether the two sets of changes actually overlapped on the same lines or not. They mostly didn't. One file — the same file both an incoming commit and the local work happened to touch — merged completely cleanly with no human intervention needed at all, once it was actually checked rather than assumed to be a problem. A separate `.gitignore` file, edited independently on both sides in almost the same spot, produced the one real, if genuinely trivial, conflict of the whole night, resolved by keeping both additions rather than guessing which one looked more current.

While all of that was still unresolved, a different and far more urgent problem surfaced: every single test label run through the live, deployed build that night came back flagged as non-compliant, on every field, with no exceptions. This was deliberately separated from the still-broken local backend integration immediately, because the two had nothing to do with each other — the live, deployed build had never depended on the local, unpushed login work, so whatever was producing garbage compliance verdicts on the actual demo surface was a frontend problem the team could not afford to leave for later, unlike the login work, which nobody outside this one machine could even see yet. The actual cause, once the raw OCR output was read directly instead of assumed, was specific and genuinely alarming: labels that were unambiguously, entirely written in English were coming back with stray Devanagari characters scattered through them that had no business existing anywhere on the source image at all. Two separate effects were pulled apart here rather than treated as one problem, because conflating them would have led to the wrong fix: an outright hallucination, where a combined-script recognition mode invented Hindi glyphs out of English letterforms that weren't even visually close to anything Devanagari; and, independently, a real, measurable degradation of the English recognition itself, a documented cost of asking one classifier to hold two full scripts' worth of possibilities open for every single glyph rather than committing to one.

The honest path was harder than the easy one, and it was named as exactly that at the time. Dropping the recognition setting to English-only would have fixed every symptom sitting in front of the team that night. It would also have quietly, silently killed multilingual label reading — one of this project's four founding differentiators, stated on the deck itself, decided at the very start of the project — without the team ever actually choosing to give it up. The fix that was designed instead, though it never made it into working code before the pitch arrived, was to run each detected text region through both languages separately and keep whichever pass returned the higher confidence for that specific region — preserving a genuinely bilingual label's real Hindi content while giving a purely English region the benefit of an undiluted, single-script read. That design got overtaken before it could be built: a teammate's own, separately developed fix landed first, justified only by the claim that it had worked against real data on his end — a claim pushed back on directly, by name, as the identical unverified pattern that had already once produced a merged pull request whose fully-green test claim turned out to hold on exactly one machine. Reading the actual diff that landed showed it touched genuine, real improvements to text cleanup and field-alias matching, and never once touched the language setting itself. Both things were true and unrelated to each other at once: the field-extraction fix genuinely helped on text that was already recognized cleanly, and the underlying hallucination on this specific set of test images sat completely untouched underneath it, an open loop that went into the pitch unresolved.

Underneath all of this, the dashboard work from Part 9 still needed testing, and the login screen — now genuinely requiring a real backend that this particular machine still couldn't reliably reach — stood directly in the way of testing screens that had nothing to do with the backend at all. The workaround was small and deliberately narrow: a short script, typed directly into the browser's own developer console, that wrote an already-logged-in state straight into the same local database the login screen itself reads from on every page load — nothing more than the exact record a successful login would have written, placed there by hand instead. It never called the real login function, never touched a network request, and never went anywhere near the shared backend at all; it only ever touched the one local check that decides whether to show the login form or skip past it. Used once to get past the login screen for testing, then deliberately cleared the same way afterward — the same local record deleted by hand — so nothing was left behind that could confuse a later, real test of the actual login flow.

Somewhere in the middle of the same night, the login-and-session work that had been sitting locally, unpushed, since Part 10 finally got pushed. The reasoning, at the time, was straightforward: holding it back had been protecting a login flow that was broken for an environmental reason — no backend reachable from this one machine — not a defect in the code itself, and continuing to hold it back wasn't accomplishing anything further. It reached the shared branch, and the connected deployment picked it up automatically. What came back immediately, and had nothing to do with login at all, was that the deployed build failed to compile: the small backend-facing client file every one of those newly pushed changes depended on had, it turned out, never actually been committed to the repository in the first place, despite sitting right there on the local disk the entire time the code that imported it was being written and tested. It had worked flawlessly, every time, on the one machine where the file genuinely existed on disk regardless of what version control thought was tracked — and it broke completely, immediately, the moment a clean checkout tried to build from what was actually committed. One more small commit, adding the missing file, fixed the build, and the deployed build turned green again. A harder fact sat right behind that fix, though, and it was said plainly rather than left implied: even with the build working again, a real visitor to that live URL still could not have logged in successfully, because nothing in the deployed configuration had ever been told a real backend's address to actually call.

## Part 13 — The result, and what came after

The internal round happened, and the team did not advance past it.

The judge looked at the live prototype and asked to see nothing else — not the deck that four days of work had gone into, not the research slide, none of it. Four pieces of concrete, specific feedback came back from that one review: that the GPS capture, built on a plain browser location call, does not meet the accuracy and tamper-resistance a real government field-inspection workflow would actually require, and that the right answer likely involves reading location directly from the device's own hardware layer rather than through a browser API at all; that an evidence photo in a report should sit larger, in its own dedicated space, rather than tucked at the bottom of the screen; that a barcode scanner — an acknowledged, deliberately deferred item on this project's own list since the very first session — should actually get built; and, raised directly and pushed on in the room, that shipping this as an installable web app rather than a native mobile application was read as choosing lower development cost over the accuracy a government customer, who was told outright does not need that trade made on its behalf, actually needs.

The result landed the same day it was announced, and it landed as a full stop on something that had been running, without a break, since the twenty-fourth of August. That gap — between what had actually been put into this, five straight weeks of real effort, real research, real testing, and what came back from one afternoon's decision by one person — is worth stating honestly rather than smoothing over, because the instinct to smooth it over showed up immediately and was worth naming for exactly what it was: telling yourself "at least we got experience" is not comfort, it's an insult dressed as comfort, when the entire reason this round of the project looked nothing like the last one — thin, rushed, barely understood by the very people who built it — was that experience was never actually the goal this time. If learning alone had been the point, there had never been any need to attach real stakes to it at all; a person could learn the exact same things anywhere, with nothing riding on the outcome. The stakes were the whole reason this year's effort looked different, and pretending afterward that the stakes never mattered was its own kind of dishonesty.

What actually followed the result, in the hours immediately after it, was a real and unresolved argument with the idea of stopping. The instinct to keep going was named plainly as a stubborn one, not dressed up as pure resolve — a genuine, admitted tension between calling that same persistence a virtue in one breath and calling it "a problem with me" in the next, both held at once, without either one winning outright. Underneath that sat a harder, quieter fear: having been the one who had pushed the rest of the team hardest through five weeks of real effort, and now having nothing external left to point to as a reason for anyone else to keep going, there was a real, named dread of hearing the team's honest reaction reduced to a single sentence — that all that effort had bought nothing worth the cost. That fear was recognized, at the time, as something being pre-written in advance of anyone actually saying it, and set aside deliberately in favor of an actual conversation rather than a rehearsed one, and in favor of an explicit, genuine offer to every teammate: whoever wanted to stop, could stop, without needing to justify it, without any grinding-through-the-night obligation reattached to keep going given the very real setback that had just landed, and whoever wanted the project to continue, could keep exactly what they already had, exactly as it already stood, with nothing about their own work touched without their say-so — a boundary already being kept in practice, not just promised, going back to the earliest backend reviews, where nothing in a teammate's own code had ever been altered without asking first. If the answer came back that nobody wanted to continue, the plan was to ask permission to take a personal copy of the whole codebase and keep going alone rather than either force the team's hand or let the project simply stop existing because momentum alone had run out.

Alongside all of that, real, concrete research went into whether this needed to be the end of the road at all — not as a way of avoiding the loss, but because the actual underlying regulatory problem hadn't gone anywhere. That research surfaced something genuinely worth knowing: the national government had, independently of this project entirely, just launched its own unified digital platform for legal metrology services — registration, licensing, verification, enforcement, all of it — meaning the specific gap this project had spent five weeks trying to close was, right now, a live area of real government investment, not a hypothetical one invented to justify a hackathon entry. No open, separate competition specifically shaped around this exact problem turned up in the search that followed, which was reported back honestly rather than papered over with something that looked promising but wasn't real. What did remain, concretely: asking the institution directly whether any flexibility existed in which team it sent forward, framed as an honest question about process rather than a grievance about the outcome; treating the underlying tool as something worth finishing for its own sake regardless of whether any further competition ever opened around it, since a real government reform effort aimed at the same problem this year makes the work relevant on its own terms; and, separately, actually addressing every piece of the judge's feedback rather than only the ones that stung least.

That last piece produced real, same-day technical work. On the location question: confirmed that a browser's plain location API and a native, hardware-level GNSS reading are not simply the same accuracy delivered through two different doors — a native layer can read raw multi-constellation satellite data, fuse it with a phone's other sensors for a stronger fix in weak signal, resist the kind of mock-location spoofing a browser can't meaningfully guard against, and log continuously in the background in a way a browser tab reliably cannot — and that a full native rebuild wasn't the only path there, since a native shell wrapped around the existing code, adding native location and camera plugins on top of it, was a real, smaller middle option worth weighing against a ground-up rewrite. On the CV-accuracy question, honestly harder to close before any deadline: real research into an image-processing pipeline that reads a real image through preprocessing steps — deskewing, contrast correction, cropping to the label itself before running recognition on it at all — before recognition ever runs on it, and into whether a different, purpose-built recognition engine could outperform a bare, unfine-tuned Tesseract on the specific, messy reality of real Indian packaging, acknowledging plainly that model choice alone would help less than actually building or finding a real dataset of real labels to tune against, which the time remaining might not allow. And a genuinely personal thread ran underneath both of these: a half-finished, paused attempt at learning the native-app toolchain that would be needed for any of this, begun before the current academic term started and set down once it did, now being weighed again — not as a settled plan, but as a real, open question about whether to pick it back up.

As of this writing, no team-wide decision has been made about which of those paths, if any, actually gets built. What has continued, deliberately, in the meantime, is the ordinary discipline this whole project has run on since Part 1: individual portions of the work kept moving forward exactly as if the project were still active, on the understanding that a real amount of undocumented progress accumulating quietly, with commit messages going stale and nobody able to see what had actually changed or why, was its own kind of dishonesty — and that writing this account down properly, in full, in order, was worth doing whether or not another competition round ever comes.

## Where things actually stand, now

The core loop from Part 6 — camera to verdict, entirely on-device, no signal required — is unchanged and still works. On top of it: a real backend now exists in this same repository, under `backend/`, with real authentication, a real if still incomplete sessions-and-products schema, and real offline-sync conflict handling, reviewed and independently live-tested against a real database more than once, with every remaining gap tracked openly rather than hidden. The frontend now attempts a real login against that backend first, and falls back to continuing offline, exactly as this project's own founding principle demands, if that call can't be reached. A complete official-side dashboard exists — summary statistics, violation trends, a filterable drill-down table, unrestricted search, a real per-item report viewer, per-officer activity, and PDF export of both a filtered set and the whole dataset — built and verified in full, deliberately against a synthetic dataset shaped to match the real backend's contracts, with the swap to live data still ahead of it rather than behind it. A full six-slide pitch deck exists, researched and corrected with the same evidence-first standard as the code. A real, still-unfixed bug sits in the multilingual OCR path, named honestly rather than papered over, with a real fix designed and waiting to be built. And the project did not advance past its first internal review — for two specific, named, previously-acknowledged trade-offs, not for any failure in the underlying idea or the underlying work — and what happens next with any of it is, as of today, still an open and genuinely unresolved question.
